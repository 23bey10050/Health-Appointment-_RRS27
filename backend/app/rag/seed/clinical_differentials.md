# Clinical Differential Frameworks (Doctor Reference)

> Decision support only. These are standard differential frameworks for common presenting
> complaints, intended to prompt consideration during triage and emergency workup generation —
> not a diagnostic algorithm and not a substitute for clinical judgment or examination.

## Acute chest pain

Time-critical considerations to exclude first, before working through the broader differential:
acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, and
oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave syndrome). Features that raise concern for a cardiac cause include
pain radiating to the arm or jaw, associated diaphoresis, dyspnoea, and pain unrelated to
palpation or movement. Pleuritic pain that worsens with inspiration and is reproducible on
palpation is more suggestive of a musculoskeletal or pleuritic cause, but this alone does not
exclude a cardiac or embolic cause in a patient with risk factors. Consider also: costochondritis,
gastro-oesophageal reflux, panic attack, and pneumonia. Risk factors that should raise the index
of suspicion for ACS include age, diabetes, hypertension, smoking history, and family history of
early cardiac disease.

## Acute breathlessness

Consider: acute asthma or COPD exacerbation, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, heart failure
decompensation, anaphylaxis, pneumothorax, and upper airway obstruction. Sudden onset favours
pneumothorax or PE; gradual onset over days favours an infective or heart-failure cause. Stridor
suggests upper airway obstruction and is a time-critical finding. Wheeze with a known asthma or
COPD history supports an exacerbation, but a first presentation of wheeze in an older adult should
not be assumed to be asthma without considering heart failure or PE.

## Acute abdominal pain

Location narrows but does not confirm the differential. Right lower quadrant pain: appendicitis,
ovarian pathology, ectopic pregnancy in a patient of reproductive age. Right upper quadrant:
biliary colic, cholecystitis, hepatitis. Epigastric: peptic ulcer disease, pancreatitis, cardiac
pain referred to the epigastrium — an inferior MI can present as epigastric pain, particularly in
diabetic or elderly patients. Diffuse pain with a rigid, guarded abdomen suggests peritonitis and
is a surgical emergency regardless of the underlying cause. Always consider ectopic pregnancy in
any patient of reproductive age with lower abdominal pain, and obtain a pregnancy status early.

## Headache

Most headaches presenting to a clinic are primary (migraine, tension-type). Features that should
prompt urgent consideration of a secondary cause: thunderclap onset (suggesting subarachnoid
haemorrhage), new headache in a patient over 50, headache with fever and neck stiffness
(suggesting meningitis), headache with new focal neurological deficit, and headache that is
worse on waking or with Valsalva (suggesting raised intracranial pressure). A headache described
as "the worst headache of my life" warrants urgent same-day evaluation, not routine booking.

## Fever

In adults, most fevers are self-limiting viral illness. Red flags: fever with a rash that doesn't
blanch under pressure, fever with neck stiffness or photophobia, fever with confusion, fever in an
immunocompromised patient, and fever persisting beyond 5 to 7 days without an identified source.
In infants under 3 months, any fever above 38°C is treated as a potential emergency regardless of
how well the infant otherwise appears, because clinical signs of serious infection are unreliable
at this age.

## Syncope and loss of consciousness

Distinguish vasovagal syncope (typically preceded by prodrome — nausea, sweating, tunnel vision,
and rapid full recovery) from cardiac syncope (sudden, without warning, especially during exertion
or while supine) and from seizure (post-ictal confusion, tongue biting, incontinence). Cardiac
syncope carries a materially higher short-term risk and warrants urgent cardiac workup. Syncope
during exercise is a red flag for a structural cardiac cause until proven otherwise.

## Altered mental status

Consider the reversible causes systematically: hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, infection (especially
urinary tract infection or pneumonia in the elderly, which can present as confusion without fever),
medication effects or interactions, electrolyte derangement, and intracranial causes (stroke,
bleed, raised pressure). A rapid finger-stick glucose should be checked early in any patient with
altered mental status, since hypoglycaemia is rapidly reversible and easily missed.
