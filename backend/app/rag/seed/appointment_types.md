# Appointment Types at City Care Clinic

This is the reference the voice agent uses to work out what kind of appointment a
patient actually needs, how long it takes, and what to tell them to bring or do
beforehand. When a patient's description fits more than one type, ask rather than
guess.

## New patient consultation

For anyone who has not been seen at the clinic before, or has not been seen in
over 24 months. Allow 30 minutes rather than the usual 20, because registration
and history-taking take longer than a follow-up.

Ask for: full name, date of birth, phone number, current medications, known
allergies, and any ongoing conditions. Tell them to bring photo ID, insurance or
scheme details if they have them, and any previous prescriptions or discharge
summaries.

## Follow-up consultation

For a patient returning about a problem already discussed with the same doctor,
usually within 8 weeks. Standard 20-minute slot. Try to book with the same doctor
who saw them originally; if that doctor is unavailable within the timeframe the
patient needs, offer a colleague in the same specialisation and say clearly that
it will be a different doctor.

Ask what has changed since the last visit, whether they finished the medication
they were given, and whether any new symptoms have appeared.

## Chronic disease review

Scheduled reviews for conditions like diabetes, hypertension, asthma, thyroid
disorders, and heart failure. Usually every 3 to 6 months. Allow 30 minutes.

Ask them to bring their home readings (blood sugar log, blood pressure diary,
peak flow readings) and their current medication boxes. If bloods are due, they
should come fasting for a morning appointment — see Visit Preparation.

## Prescription refill / repeat medication

Short 10-minute slot for stable patients on long-term medication who need a
repeat. Not appropriate if the patient reports new symptoms, side effects, or
wants a dose change — those need a normal consultation, so re-route.

Ask which medication, what strength, when they last had it reviewed, and whether
anything has changed.

## Vaccination

Routine childhood immunisations, adult boosters, seasonal flu, travel vaccines.
15-minute slot. Pediatrics for children, General Medicine for adults.

Ask the patient's age, which vaccine, and whether they have had any previous
reaction to a vaccine. For travel vaccines, ask the destination and travel date —
some schedules need to start 4 to 6 weeks ahead.

## Health check / preventive screening

Annual or pre-employment health checks, well-woman and well-man checks. 45
minutes including bloods. Best booked as an early morning fasting slot.

Ask whether they need a specific certificate or format for an employer, since
that changes which panel is ordered.

## Test results review

Short 15-minute appointment to go through results already on file. Only book this
if the results have actually been reported — if they were taken in the last 48
hours, tell the patient they may not be back yet and offer a slightly later slot.

## Procedure / minor surgery

Wound dressing, suture removal, abscess drainage, joint injection, skin lesion
removal, ear syringing. Duration varies from 20 to 45 minutes, so confirm with
the doctor's schedule rather than assuming.

Ask what procedure was advised and by whom. Tell them to arrange someone to
accompany them home if sedation is involved.

## Antenatal visit

Routine pregnancy care. Frequency increases through pregnancy: monthly to 28
weeks, fortnightly to 36 weeks, then weekly. 30-minute slots, Obstetrics.

Ask how many weeks pregnant, whether this is a first pregnancy, and whether there
have been any complications flagged so far. Any bleeding, severe abdominal pain,
reduced fetal movements, or severe headache with visual change is an emergency
escalation, not a routine antenatal booking.

## Mental health consultation

Longer 45-minute slot. Book with Psychiatry, or General Medicine for an initial
conversation if the patient prefers. Be unhurried and non-judgemental when taking
these bookings.

Any mention of self-harm, suicidal thinking, or not feeling safe is an immediate
escalation to the crisis pathway, regardless of how calmly it is expressed.

## Second opinion

Patient wants another view on a diagnosis or treatment plan made elsewhere. 30
minutes. Ask them to bring all reports, imaging, and prescriptions from the
original provider — without those the visit is much less useful, and it is worth
saying so when booking.

## Teleconsultation

Video or phone consultation for follow-ups, prescription reviews, and results
discussions. Not suitable for anything requiring physical examination, or for a
first visit about a new physical symptom.

Confirm a working phone number and note that a video link is emailed 15 minutes
before the slot.

## Emergency / walk-in

Not booked through the normal availability system. When a red flag is detected,
the emergency pathway takes over: the patient is given emergency numbers
immediately, asked whether an ambulance is needed, and an emergency slot is
reserved at the nearest hospital with an emergency department. The on-call doctor
is paged. Never place an emergency into a routine appointment slot.
